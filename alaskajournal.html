Travel Journal

Heaven on Earth

Alaska · Anchorage · Denali · Seward · Juneau

More than 75 miles on foot, untouched landscape at every turn, and nature I could hardly believe. Just nature and us. Here's how it went.

AnchorageWhere the Planes Land on Water

Flew AS1546, ORD to ANC, 10:00 AM to 1:42 PM. Landed, grabbed the rental. Threw the luggage in the trunk and we were off.

First stop wasn't even planned. Right by the airport we pulled over to watch planes take off and land on water. That's Lake Hood Seaplane Base, the busiest seaplane base in the world, something like 190 flights a day in summer with around 800 planes based there. There's a museum next door, the Alaska Aviation Museum, but we skipped it this time.

We picked up some sandwiches and a coffee and got going.

Tony Knowles Coastal Trail, Point Woronzof section

This trail runs 11 miles one way from downtown out to Kincaid Park, hugging Cook Inlet the whole way. The Point Woronzof stretch looks right out at jets landing and taking off from Ted Stevens airport. We walked about 3.5 miles one way and saw a moose. We walked through Earthquake Park, where a whole neighborhood slid into the sea during the 1964 Good Friday earthquake, magnitude 9.2, still the strongest ever recorded in North America. Strange to walk through something like that.

Flattop Mountain

Drove up into Chugach State Park for this one. Flattop is the pretty famous for climbing. From the Glen Alps trailhead it's 1.5 miles one way and about 1,280 feet of climbing to the plateau, and the last push to the summit gets steep and rocky. We tried part of the trail and walked back may be after 1/2 mile journey. Then walked around the viewpoint, totaling more than a mile. Still got our first scrambling in.

We walked 9-plus miles between the coastal trail, sea plane area, and Flattop. Our legs earned that Indian food.

Dinner

Stumbled onto a packed Indian restaurant on the way back to the hotel. Food was delicious. Collapsed after.

Anchorage to DenaliChasing Denali Through the Rain

The plan was ambitious: drive up to Talkeetna and take a flightseeing tour with K2 Aviation out to Denali. We had an 11 AM check-in, so we were up early again, on the road while it was still gray. It stayed that way all morning, low clouds, fog, a little rain. Not the sky you want when you're hoping to fly around the tallest mountain in North America.

We broke up the drive with two stops, both just north of Anchorage.

Thunderbird Falls

A short trail off the Glenn Highway in Chugach State Park, about a mile each way. It climbs a little at first, then levels off through birch forest along the edge of the Eklutna River canyon. At the end there's a viewing deck looking out at the falls, which drop close to 200 feet. The name comes from an old Native legend about the Thunderbird, a giant bird said to bring the thunder. The rain had the falls running full and loud, so for once the weather helped.

Mirror Lake

Just a quick pull-off farther up the highway. We got out, walked around, took it in. A calm little lake with a picnic area right off the road, good for stretching your legs before getting back in the car.

Talkeetna, and the flight that didn't happen

We reached Talkeetna right around 11. Turned out they'd let us know earlier that the Denali flight was canceled, and we just hadn't caught the message. No surprise given the sky. Denali makes its own weather and is only visible about a third of the time in summer, and today it stayed hidden.

Talkeetna is a small place with a big reputation. It sits where three rivers meet, the Chulitna, the Susitna, and the Talkeetna, and it's the town most Denali climbers launch from before flying out to base camp. We headed down to the riverfront and walked the shore for a while, watching the water braid across the gravel bars.

Mahay's Jetboat Adventure

Since we were already there, we booked Mahay's Jetboat Adventure. These boats have run the rivers out of Talkeetna since 1975, built to skim over water only inches deep, which gets you back into country you couldn't reach otherwise. Partway through they pulled ashore for a short walk out to a replica Dena'ina camp and an old trapper's cabin. Fast, loud, cold spray off the water, and a better plan B than sitting around disappointed.

For lunch we finished the leftover Indian food from last night.

The drive north to Denali

After the boat we pointed the car north. We stopped at both Denali viewpoints on the Parks Highway, south first, then north. The south viewpoint sits about 40 miles from the mountain, over the wide, braided Chulitna River, and a lot of people call it the best view of Denali from the road. There's a short trail there, half a mile out and back, with a steeper path branching off that climbs higher, so we walked that too. On a clear day you can line up Denali with Mount Foraker and Mount Hunter from here, but the mountain stayed mostly in cloud for us. We stopped anyway, and pulled over for photos plenty of other times when something caught our eye.

Horseshoe Lake Trail

Once we were in the Denali area we did the full Horseshoe Lake Trail. It starts on high ground near the park entrance with an overlook, then drops steeply to the lake, and from there we walked the whole loop around the water and spent time down by the river. The lake is named for its horseshoe, or oxbow, shape, and there's a big beaver dam and lodge along the way, with the Nenana River running nearby. Only a couple of miles round trip, but the steep climb back up out of the basin at the end had us feeling it.

Dinner

Can't remember what we ate. Probably just the sandwiches we'd picked up the first day, whatever was left. Long day, and by then food was more fuel than event.

Where we stayed

Our base for Denali was a lodge right on the Nenana River, about a mile from the park entrance. The room backed onto the river, so you could step out the door and there was the Nenana going by in the backyard, that steady rush of water the whole time. Paved paths run along the river bluff too, so you can walk the water's edge straight from the room.

Denali National ParkThe Mountain Finally Shows Itself

The day we actually got into the park, on the Tundra Wilderness Tour. We were on the bus around 7:30, out of the Denali Bus Depot with a naturalist driver narrating. There's one gravel road into the whole six-million-acre park, and private cars can only go the first 15 miles. After that it's buses, so a tour is really the only way to get deep in. This year the road is closed at Mile 43 for an ongoing landslide at Pretty Rocks, so we rode out to about there and turned around near the old Murie Cabin at the East Fork River.

And then the thing that didn't happen the day before happened. The clouds broke and we got a full, clear view of Denali. After the canceled flight and the mountain hiding all day, that was the one we'd been after. We also saw moose, caribou, and a few birds. The bus had a snack pack and water. Back to the start around 1 PM.

At the depot we grabbed good coffee at the bus shelter, ate a little, and then, against better judgment, went straight out for a long trail.

Mount Healy Overlook Trail

No joke, this one. Rated strenuous, somewhere between 4.5 and 5.4 miles round trip depending where you start, about 1,700 feet of climbing.For us it turned to be 6 mile roundtrip. It goes up steeply through spruce forest and breaks out above the treeline into alpine tundra, and the overlook at the top opens up 360-degree views over the Nenana River Valley and the ridges around it. We finished the whole thing. My god, I was tired. Worth it, but my legs were done.

Denali Visitor Center trails

After Mount Healy we still poked around the visitor center and walked a few of the short loops there. Maybe a mile, easy and flat after what we'd just done. A good way to cool the legs off.

Two big things back to back. Tiring day.

Dinner

Found a pizza place and had pizza and a drink, well earned. Then back to the room and straight to sleep, listening sound of the river running behind us. Next day was a big one, a long haul south to Seward.

Denali to SewardThe Long Road to Seward

Big driving day. Plan was to reach Seward around 8 PM, but with all the stops we didn't roll in until 10:40. It mattered, because the front desk closed at 11. Miss that and we'd have had to call ahead and get someone to come in and check us in. We made it with twenty minutes to spare.

The drive down there is heaven on earth.

Denali viewpoints, take two

Heading south out of the park we stopped at the viewpoints again, north then south. This time the mountain was fully out. After Day 2 gave us nothing but cloud, there it was, all 20,310 feet of Denali in the open. We walked that short trail at the south viewpoint again, since now there was something to see from the top. Somewhere along here we finished the leftover pizza.

Beluga Point

Past Anchorage, onto the Seward Highway, the road runs right along Turnagain Arm, and we pulled off at Beluga Point. It's named for the beluga whales that chase salmon up the arm, and it's a good spot to catch the bore tide, the single wave that rolls in when the tide turns in this narrow, shallow inlet. Look up at the cliffs above the road and you'll sometimes see Dall sheep picking their way across.

Bird Point

A little farther down we stopped at Bird Point, another pullout on the same stretch, with a paved path and wide views up and down Turnagain Arm.

Portage Glacier and Portage Lake

Then we turned off toward Portage. The whole valley is full of glaciers, and the big one is Portage Glacier, at the far end of Portage Lake, a glacial lake that runs something like 600 feet deep. The glacier has pulled back so far over the last century, more than three miles since 1914, that you can't see it from the shore parking lot anymore.

We stopped at the lake near the visitor center and took many pictures of the beautiful lake.

On the way to Seward we were thinking of stopping at Alaska Wildlife Conservation Center, because of time constraints we could not squeeze in.

The Whittier tunnel and Whittier

This part wasn't planned. On a whim we drove out to Whittier, and getting there is half the experience. The only road in runs through the Anton Anderson Memorial Tunnel, two and a half miles long, the longest highway tunnel in North America and the longest on the continent shared by cars and trains. It's a single lane cut through Maynard Mountain, with railroad tracks down the middle of the road, so traffic runs one direction at a time on a schedule, into Whittier on the half hour and back out on the hour. Driving through that narrow, dark, dripping tunnel with train tracks under the tires is one of the strangest drives. This fully brought back my memory from a decade-old trip to Alaska. I had gone through this tunnel before, and it snapped back as a flashback.

Horsetail Falls Trail, Whittier

Whittier sits at the head of a deep fjord ringed by snowy peaks, and of course we found another trail. I don't remember the name, but I'm fairly sure it was the Horsetail Falls Trail, the one that switchbacks steeply up through thick forest on boardwalks and ends at a square wooden platform high above town. From that platform straight down is the harbor, the breakwater, and Passage Canal with the mountains stacked up all around. Beautiful climb.

Getting to Seward

After all that we still had to get to Seward, back through the tunnel and south down the highway. We pulled in at 10:40 PM, legs done, but we'd beaten the cutoff.

Where we stayed

For the next two nights we were in Seward. Not our favorite. Pricey when we booked, and the room didn't match the price, very old and extremely small room with very high temp inside rooms and no fans/ac. We got in around 11 anyway, so dinner was just the leftover sandwiches we'd picked up.

SewardWhales, Fjords, and a Calving Glacier

The day we'd really come to Seward for. A full-day cruise with Major Marine Tours into Kenai Fjords National Park, the better part of nine hours on the water. The boat pushed out of the harbor, down through Resurrection Bay and into the fjords, and the wildlife kept coming, whales surfacing, birds everywhere, so many Puffins, sea lions on the rocks. Near a tidewater glacier (tidewater glaciers are glaciers that flow from mountains or ice sheets directly into the sea) we watched it calve, a slab of ice breaking off the face and crashing into the sea with a crack you feel as much as hear. The water around the glacier was full of floating ice. Lunch came with the boat. Back to shore around 5.

That could've been the day. It wasn't. We drove straight out to Exit Glacier, the one part of Kenai Fjords National Park you can reach by road, about twenty minutes from Seward. It comes down off the Harding Icefield, which feeds something like three dozen glaciers. We did the trail to the glacier viewpoint, a little over three miles. The part that stays with you is the walk in, where markers show the years the glacier's edge used to reach, the dates creeping further back as the ice shrinks. The glacier got its name from being the "exit" the first team used coming off the icefield in 1968. I could see the difference between my last trip and this one, the glacier had moved back close to a mile.

Dinner

Found a Mexican place in town and the food was really good. Packed. After, we walked along the Seward coast for a while, then called it.

Seward to JuneauFrom the Road to the Air

Travel day. Return the rental, catch a flight, change cities. But we couldn't just drive straight through.

Seward to Anchorage

The drive back north was slow in the best way, full of stops. We pulled off for a mile-long trail down to one of the creeks along the highway, just to stretch. Then we swung into Girdwood to the Mount Alyeska tram station. Alyeska is the biggest ski resort in the state, and the tram climbs about 2,300 feet to the top of Mount Alyeska, with views over Turnagain Arm and as many as seven hanging glaciers in the Chugach. We wanted to ride it, but the timing wouldn't work with our flight, so we saw the station and moved on.

Anchorage

Back in Anchorage we had a lunch buffet at an Indian restaurant, exactly the heavy meal we needed. Then we returned the car, and the road-trip half of the trip was over. Off to the airport for the flight to Juneau.

Juneau, and the fastest car rental ever

Landed in Juneau and picked up a car from the rental company, the smoothest rental checkout I've had. Walk to the counter, grab the keys, sign in two or three spots, in the car.

Airport Dike Trail

We went out to the trail behind the Juneau airport, the Airport Dike Trail, and did close to five miles. It's a flat path along the airport dike through the Mendenhall Wetlands. The dike was built to hold the water for the floatplane pond.

Dinner, or not

We skipped dinner. That Indian buffet had been such a big late lunch that neither of us was hungry.

Where we stayed

Hotel was near the airport. Expensive again, but fine this time. Next day we'd finally be out on the water, whale watching.

JuneauHumpbacks and Upside-Down Trees

Whale Watching and Wildlife Quest

Our morning was booked with True Alaskan Tours for their Whale Watching and Wildlife Quest, an 8:30 start. We parked at the AJ lot near the port and a guide bused us out to the departure area. She was great, full of Juneau facts the whole ride. Some of the "streets" here are actually staircases up the hillside, and the city shovels the snow off them in winter. The highways don't really go anywhere, because Juneau is the one state capital you can't drive to, boat or plane only. Then we got out on the water, and there they were, humpback whales, right off the boat. Back around 1 PM.

On this ride we saw so many bald Eagle. I have never seen that many balk eagle in my entire life. They were all around , on trees / electirc poles, top of building. Fun to see so many in one place.

Glacier Gardens Rainforest Adventure

Next was Glacier Gardens Rainforest Adventure, an hour-long guided tour riding a covered cart up through the forest. It sits inside the Tongass National Forest, the biggest national forest in the country, and it's a temperate rainforest, not what most people picture when they think Alaska. The thing everyone comes for is the "Flower Towers," storm-felled trees flipped upside down and replanted so the roots point up, then filled with flowers spilling out the top. The tour climbs to a viewpoint a few hundred feet up with a wide look over Juneau.

Perseverance Trail

Then the day's long walk. We took the Perseverance Trail, which starts above downtown Juneau where Basin Road ends. It's an old mining route, said to be one of the first roads ever built in Alaska, running up the Gold Creek valley past Ebner Falls and the remains of the old gold works. We went past the main trail and did the Red Mill extension and the overlook of the Glory Hole, a huge open pit the miners blasted straight down into the mountain, hundreds of feet deep with sheer walls.

Dinner

We found an Indian restaurant in town with a good story behind it. It's run by a woman who moved up from Ohio about twenty years ago, because she fell in love with Juneau and stayed. The food would've been worth it on its own, but the story made it better.

JuneauA Glacier and Too Many Trails

We were out early, mostly hoping to see bears. The Mendenhall Glacier area is one of the best places around Juneau to catch them, so we wanted the early odds.

Mendenhall Glacier and its trails

The glacier runs about thirteen miles down out of the Juneau Icefield, a 1,500-square-mile sheet of ice left from the last ice age, and it ends at Mendenhall Lake, where chunks of blue ice float out front. It sits only about twelve miles from downtown.

We tried to do all of it. We started at the visitor center and walked the Trail of Time, a short interpretive loop that shows how the land has grown back in the years since the glacier pulled away from each spot. We took the Photo Point path for the straight-on view of the glacier's face across the lake, and the Steep Creek boardwalk, which runs along a salmon-spawning stream and is the best place around here to see bears when the fish are running. Then Nugget Falls Trail, an easy two miles out to Nugget Falls, close to 400 feet tall, dropping right beside the glacier. From there the longer, steeper East Glacier Trail for a higher angle on the ice, plus the Moraine Ecology Trail through the forest and rubble the glacier left behind. And then, because we weren't done, the Dredge Lakes trails just outside the main area, a quiet maze of ponds and woods where the old gold dredges used to work.

Come evening we drove over and walked the Auke Lake Trail, an easy shoreline loop near the university where the glacier reflects across the water and floatplanes sit parked in the lake grass. From there we kept driving north, following Glacier Highway out to where it ends. Locals call this stretch "out the road," and it runs about forty miles from downtown before dead-ending at Echo Cove, because Juneau doesn't connect to anywhere else by road. We stopped at a string of pullouts and beaches on the way out, including a bird watching spot, but by then it had gone gray and cloudy and the birds weren't showing.

Dinner

By the time we were hungry it was around 8:30, and most of the restaurants near the hotel had already closed. We ended up with pizza from an arcade place nearby, which after a day like that tasted fine.

JuneauUp Through the Rainforest to the Cabin

The main goal was the Dan Moller Trail all the way to the cabin, but we made a good stop first.

Birds on the way

We pulled off at a birdwatching area Mendenhall Glacier forelands along Mendenhall Lake yield ponds to big puddles where a variety of species can be spotted. Eagles everywhere, more than we could count, and once we started walking, all kinds of other species turned up. We put in a couple of miles poking around. After the washed-out, cloudy bird stop the evening before, this made up for it.

Dan Moller Trail

The trailhead is over on Douglas Island, across the channel from downtown, about three miles up to the Dan Moller Cabin, gaining around 1,800 feet. It's a real climb, but it hardly ever feels like one. Most of the trail runs on raised wooden planks over open muskeg meadows, and the rainforest and the sheer variety of plants keep pulling your attention off your legs. There are things growing out there I couldn't put into words. You have to go stand in it. One of the most scenic trails we did the whole trip, and the cabin at the top, in its alpine bowl, is a good place to catch your breath.

Downtown Juneau

After that we went into the city. We stopped by the whale first, Tahku, a life-size bronze humpback caught mid-breach in a little waterfront park, around 25 feet tall, in a fountain rigged so the water sheets off it like it's really surfacing. From there we walked to Marine Park and picked up the Totem Pole Trail. We counted and photographed all twelve of the totem poles along it. Each one is carved by a different Native master artist and tells a story from a particular clan, part of a Sealaska Heritage project along the downtown seawalk. We wandered past a few other downtown spots, did a little shopping, and headed back.

JuneauLost in the Woods on the Way Home

Last day. That night we'd fly home to Chicago by way of Seattle, so we had one more Alaska morning, and we spent it on a trail.

Outer Point Loop Trail

We drove out to North Douglas Island for the Outer Point Loop Trail. The path winds through old-growth Sitka spruce and hemlock rainforest and opens into muskeg meadows full of wildflowers, our own valley of flowers, before dropping to rocky beaches along the channel with Admiralty Island across the water. Forest, bog, and shoreline all in one walk.

The part where we got lost

Coming back is where it got interesting. The trail ducks back into the trees after the beach, and somewhere in there we lost it completely. Couldn't get our bearings, couldn't navigate, and a little panic set in, deep in the woods with no clear way out. But that's how it goes. Eventually we came out near the shoreline, saw some other people, and followed them until we were back on track. Made it. Heart rate returned to normal.

On the way to the airport

With a little time left before the flight, we squeezed in a few last stops, a couple of pullouts and beaches, and one more short river trail on our route, a quick walk along the water before we turned the car in.

And that was the trip. More miles on foot than I can count, glaciers and whales and eagles and rainforest, and one very memorable afternoon lost in the woods. Alaska, we'll be back.
